# Voice

Extracted from Henrik's own writing, not invented. Sources:

- [The answer, my friend, is blowin' in the Tailwind](https://www.larssonhenrik.com/articles/the-answer-my-friend-is-blowin-in-the-tail-wind) — the voice sample
- [Build a breadcrumb using Sanity and Next.js](https://www.larssonhenrik.com/articles/build-a-breadcrumb-using-sanity-and-next-js) — the instructional register

Read the samples at the bottom before writing anything. They matter more than the rules.

## What the voice does

- **Tells a story over time, in first person.** "In 2016 I graduated from school, applied for jobs and eventually got one a few months later." Copy starts from what he tried, not from what a product is.
- **Admits what he got wrong, and doesn't tidy it up.** "Needless to say, my CSS became a mess." "I had a look but quickly decided I was not interested." "It was never used... I'm glad for that now." This is the most distinctive thing in the voice and the easiest to drop.
- **Hedges honestly.** "with varying success", "maybe at the end of 2018 or start of 2019", "according to me", "My experience is that", "I'm not sure your CSS journey should begin with Tailwind, rather end with it". He rarely claims more than he knows.
- **Says the benefit plainly.** "it saves us a lot of time." No reframing it as insight.
- **Uses we/us for work with colleagues.** Teams are present in the writing, not abstracted away.
- **Mild, ordinary enthusiasm.** "epic documentation", "a massive win", "huge advantage". Not clever.
- **Plays only in the title.** The Dylan pun is in the headline; the body is completely plain.
- **British spelling.** realised, memorise, recognisable, prioritise.

## What to avoid — all of these were in the first drafts of these posts

- **Aphorisms.** "Content work rarely fails loudly." "A queue you can quietly lie to stops being worth reading." He never writes a line designed to be quotable.
- **"X, not Y" parallelism.** "A queue, not a scoreboard." "Proactive, not nagging." "An inbox, not a dashboard." Three of these in one post is a tell.
- **Em-dash cadence** used for rhythm — like this — repeatedly.
- **Bold-lead bullets** with a punchy two-word label, then a colon.
- **Flat certainty.** Every sentence landing as a verdict, with no "I think" or "in my experience" anywhere.
- **Sentence fragments for emphasis.** Deliberately. Like this.
- **Rule-of-three lists** where two examples would do.

## Length

Short. A LinkedIn post is ~700 characters, a Discord post ~850. One point, not two, and never
three. Everything else is a follow-up post. Three drafts of these posts were rejected for length
before this rule got written down.

## Sample — keep these in view while writing

> My approach to CSS? No approach really. I styled whatever was available (id:s/classes) to style in my main.css file. Naming conventions? As long as I had some sort of idea what I was styling, that was good enough. Needless to say, my CSS became a mess.

> At some point, maybe at the end of 2018 or start of 2019, I decided I wanted to move away from CSS frameworks entirely. [...] So instead I built a custom framework with a solid grid system, handy utility classes and beautifully named and structured SCSS and HTML, figuring this was what we were going to use the following years. It was never used, the reason being that we didn't start any new projects by then. I'm glad for that now.

> As I began to develop this portfolio during spring/early summer 2020, I decided to give Tailwind an honest try. Tailwind felt odd and "wrong" in the beginning. My HTML felt bloated compared to the BEM classes I had used before. After a while though, I realised how smooth my workflow was.

> To sum up, I'm not sure your CSS journey should begin with Tailwind, rather end with it.
